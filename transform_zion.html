What I believe

Tuesday, February 4, 2025

I believe in the restoration of the gospel of Jesus Christ. I am a believing member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints because of that simple, profound fact. I have a spiritual knowledge—a witness—of where the Book of Mormon came from and recognize the divine hand that brought it forth from the dust of forgotten history. I have a testimony of the power of the Book of Mormon, and I believe it speaks of Christ in a sacred, understandable light.

While I do not—and perhaps cannot—understand every word that proceeds from a prophet’s mouth, I have faith in the leaders of the Church. I believe they are earnestly seeking further light and knowledge that only God can provide. Through prayer and study, I’ve learned how to listen to the Holy Spirit confirm truth to me in my life, heart, and mind.

I love being part of the body of Christ and feeling the love and compassion of a ward family. I try my best to emulate my Savior’s example, and I have long loved the words of King Benjamin from Mosiah 4:15: “…ye will teach them to love one another, and to serve one another.”

Truthfully, my faith is all I have known from the start. I was born into it—into a family of varying degrees of faith—and early on I learned something deeper about the religion in which I was being raised. Even when family members ebbed and flowed in commitment, many still abounded in good works, cultivated solemn morality, and sought to expand their lives with truth and wisdom.

To me, that revealed the genuine influence of the gospel—even by proximity. In a quiet, sometimes unexpected way, this foundation has yielded fruits I could not have predicted in my youth: resilience to temptation, a willingness to look beyond certitudes, and a flexibility to adapt when faced with doubt and hardship. I remain a believing member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints because of the fruits I have seen in my life, not because I think human institutions are flawless.

I have a testimony of prayer. God answers prayers—not always in the way we expect, but always in the way we need. The mere act of opening your heart to your Maker requires humility and a surrender of control to something bigger than yourself. I hold fast to the promises of God that if I look to Him in all things, I will be guided to do good continually.

I honor and respect the priesthood with which I have been blessed in this life. I recognize that by my sex assigned at birth, I was given the responsibility to exercise this sacred power to lift others and care for the poor in spirit.

Which brings me to my next point: as I understand and live my faith, being non-binary does not exclude me from practicing, in full faith, the tenets of the gospel—or from ministering to those in need through priesthood service. I am careful to attend to current Church guidelines, retain my “he” pronouns, and do not have plans to medically transition to alleviate the dysphoria I have experienced since I was a child.

I am choosing to walk this path not primarily to make life easier, but to shine a light for Latter-day Saint individuals who experience gender dysphoria—especially those who feel alone. I walk this path as a way forward: a path of personal revelation, and a path of humility, as I lead my family alongside my wife.

If I wanted life to be easy, I would have stayed silent. I would have tamped down the emotions that rise each time I look in the mirror and see someone that I am not—someone who does not fully reflect the person I know myself to be. I would have forcefully dried the tears I cry when I experience the rigid, often painful binary culture that can exist around us. I could have done that; it has been my way for decades, and I became very good at hiding—even from myself.

But my faith in Christ and in the restoration of the gospel compels me to take up this yoke, embrace this challenge, and walk this path. I will not be alone, because in Gethsemane my Savior took upon Himself not only the sins of a world but also its sorrows, weaknesses, and struggles. Christ died for me so that I may truly live—authentically and completely.

It is now my responsibility to live my life for Him, so that through me others may come to know Him—not only the historical Jesus, but the living Christ who walks with us today.

Hello, and welcome

Saturday, February 1, 2025

Sharing this part of myself isn’t easy. In the past year, I have felt a pull on my heart to open up about who I am— who I have always been, as far as I can remember. This part of my life is now ready to be spoken aloud, even though it feels both exhilarating and unnerving.

In different seasons of my life, I chose to hide—out of comfort, safety, convenience, role requirements, and sometimes denial. But something shifts as we grow older: the facades we build begin to dissolve in the face of mortality. Over time, I realized how stuck I had become in my own head, with anxiety and depression only the tip of the iceberg. I began to see how the constant choice between authenticity and conformity can trap a person in a cognitive corner.

And the last straw was how it affected my faith in God.

Christianity can carry a tense relationship with gender, often framed through a strict male/female binary. This tension was something I wrestled with deeply. Scripture tells us that God created mankind; male and female, He created them. Over time, those who didn’t fit neat categories were often misunderstood or pushed aside. But we must remember: though God works in simplicity, I do not believe He limits the creativity of His expression—including in the realm of gender.

The same God who gave us day and night also gave us dawn and dusk—endless shades of color and light between the binaries. Marshes, bogs, wetlands—liminal places—allow delicate life to thrive where it might otherwise perish. Just as these in-between places exist in nature, I believe there are sacred in-between places within human experience as well.

I exist in that in-between space.

I believe my experience, like the experiences of those who identify strictly as male or female, occupies a meaningful place within God’s creation. And I believe that walking this road has expanded my capacity to love—across circumstance, creed, gender, race, social standing, and the many constructs that tend to separate rather than unify.

So please, be patient with me as I share. I understand the discomfort this topic can bring. I also understand that discomfort can sometimes be mistaken for certainty. I invite anyone who feels that pull toward certainty to walk with me with an open heart: listen to my story, and consider the possibility that love grows when we risk proximity.

P.S. I know this may raise questions. To help address them, I’ve written a Frequently Asked Questions section where I talk about the complexities of being non-binary and a member of the Church—how I reconcile faith and identity, what challenges I face, and what it can look like to be an ally. If you’re curious, I invite you to explore it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can someone be non-binary and a member of the Church?

Gender identity is a deeply personal and complex aspect of who we are, separate from gender expression or sexual orientation. Every individual’s experience is unique, and there is no single way to be non-binary.

For me—and for many others—being non-binary is part of our identity, much like being gay or lesbian is part of someone else’s. We can integrate this understanding of ourselves with our faith and participation in the Church.

The Church acknowledges this complexity on its website, stating:

“Some people feel their inner sense of gender does not align with their biological sex at birth. The Church does not take a position on the causes of these feelings. Some who experience these feelings identify as transgender.” — The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints
SOURCE: UNDERSTANDING_YOURSELF

I don’t quote this to “win” anything—I quote it because it acknowledges complexity. And for me, that matters. I believe there is space in the Church community for people navigating real and sometimes difficult questions about gender.

How does your identity fit within the Plan of Salvation?

Quite simply, it doesn’t appear that it fits cleanly at all. Like many aspects of faith, interpretations of gender roles and the Plan of Salvation vary. Many non-binary Latter-day Saints find ways to reconcile their understanding of these concepts with lived experience. For me, it remains a topic of ongoing study, prayer, and personal revelation.

How do you reconcile your gender identity with Church teachings on gender?

This is a deeply personal question, and the answer is unique to each individual. It often involves prayer, study, and seeking guidance from the Spirit. There is no single, prescribed way to reconcile these parts of a person’s life.

For me, it has been a process of seeking personal revelation and learning over time. While the Proclamation initially seemed to present a stark challenge, I have come to believe that God speaks to us individually and guides us on our unique paths. Through prayer and pondering, I’ve felt a strong confirmation that acknowledging my experience is part of my discipleship—not a departure from it.

I believe God loves and accepts us all, including those of us who are non-binary. I have felt the Spirit confirm that walking honestly—without unnecessary performance—can be part of the work of becoming who God wants me to be.

When did you know you were non-binary?

While the terminology didn’t exist in my very young mind, I knew that I felt different from an early age. My earliest memories of gender dysphoria came at around 5 years old.

I noted in a personal journal entry a few years ago this memory: “I’m five years old. It’s 6 am, well before school. Mom lets me watch PBS in the mornings before I go to preschool at Mrs. Wing’s house. Mr. Rogers is speaking to me. I love Mr. Rogers. He makes me feel safe and loved and ok with the world. He’s talking about the differences between boys and girls, but did I hear him say that boys could be girls? Probably not. But I go to bed that night and dream about waking up the next day a girl. That can’t be right.”

Even at that young age, I remember longing—wishing and waiting—to be transformed into a version of myself that felt more comfortable. That feeling stayed with me for many years, even when I had no words for it.

What pronouns do you use?

My pronouns are he/him/his. This is something I’ve given a lot of thought to, especially in light of the Church’s guidelines on gender and identity. While I identify as non-binary, I have chosen not to socially transition at this time. This means I continue to use the pronouns that align with my assigned sex at birth.

This decision comes from personal reflection and seeking guidance from the Spirit. I respect the counsel of Church leaders and want to navigate my path in a way that feels both authentic to me and respectful of the community I belong to.

I also want to acknowledge the complexity here: there are many perspectives within the Church, and many lived experiences. I honor those who choose to socially transition, and I respect those who do not. Ultimately, I believe it is a matter of personal revelation and following the path that feels right for each individual.

What name do you prefer?

I prefer to go by Rylee, but RyanDavid is wonderful as well.

What has your experience been like with your ward/family/friends?

I’ve been incredibly fortunate to have a supportive network of family, friends, and ward members. Everyone I’ve shared my story with has responded with grace and love.

Of course, there are times when deep understanding might be lacking—but I believe that’s where proximity and honest communication do their work. By sharing my experiences and being open to questions, I’ve found I can build bridges even with those who begin from a very different perspective.

Most importantly of all, I am lucky enough to have a kind, empathetic, and supportive wife by my side. She was willing to walk with me from the time we met. I consider her heart above all others, because dysphoria can involve real grief and loss. For that reason—and for many other personal reasons—I try to be thoughtful about how and when I share.

I’m grateful for the relationships I have, especially with my wife, and for the way they have supported me. Their love and understanding have been invaluable.

What are the biggest challenges you face?
  • Misunderstanding and Judgment: It’s not always easy for people to understand experiences different from their own, which can lead to hurtful misunderstandings or judgments. Bridging gaps takes patience and open communication.
  • Fear of Losing Relationships: One of my biggest fears is losing relationships with loved ones who might not understand or accept my experience of gender. Balancing authenticity with maintaining those connections is an ongoing challenge.
  • Safety and Acceptance: In primarily conservative areas, there can be legitimate safety concerns that come with being visibly gender-nonconforming. Finding belonging and acceptance can be challenging.
  • Internal Conflict: Balancing faith with gender-related questions can create internal conflict, requiring ongoing effort to find peace and reconciliation.

Despite these challenges, I remain committed to living honestly and finding my place within the Church and my community. I believe that through open communication, vulnerability, and a willingness to learn from one another, we can create a more loving space for everyone.

Are you planning on transitioning / have you transitioned?

This is a very personal question.

I have no plans to transition, either socially or medically. This decision comes from personal reflection, prayer, and seeking guidance from the Spirit. It is the path that feels right for me at this point in my life.

It’s important to emphasize that decisions about transitioning are deeply personal. Some non-binary people choose to medically or socially transition, while others do not. There is no single “right way” to be non-binary, and I respect the choices others make for themselves.

I believe it’s crucial to avoid assumptions about anyone’s journey and to create a space where people are treated with respect and dignity, regardless of the path they walk.

Doesn’t this mean you’re gay?

This question highlights an important distinction: gender identity and sexual orientation are separate aspects of who we are.

To answer directly: I do not experience attraction toward men.

However, a non-binary person can be gay, lesbian, bisexual, straight, or any other sexual orientation. Being non-binary doesn’t automatically determine someone’s sexual orientation.

I can only imagine how challenging it must be for those who experience same-sex attraction and also navigate complex questions about gender. The process can feel layered and heavy, and I have tremendous respect for those on that road.

How can I be a better ally?

I love this question. It shows a genuine desire to support the LGBTQ+ community. To answer, I’ll borrow a phrase from my favorite LGBTQ+ podcast: listen, learn, and love.

Here are a few ways to put that into action, especially within a faith-based context:

  • Educate Yourself: Learn terminology and lived experiences. Read books, listen to podcasts, and engage reputable resources. Understanding is a foundation of allyship.
  • Listen with Empathy: When someone shares their story, listen with an open heart and without judgment. Their experience is real, even if it differs from yours.
  • Love without Condition: Show love and respect regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity. Remember that Christ’s love extends to everyone.
  • Advocate for Inclusion: Speak up for inclusion and dignity in your ward, community, and family. Challenge prejudice. Make space for people to belong.
  • Offer Support: Be someone safe. Sometimes allyship is as simple as being steady, kind, and present.
  • Practice Patience: Understanding doesn’t always happen overnight. Be patient with yourself and others as learning unfolds.
  • Pray for Guidance: Seek the Spirit to know how you can best show love to those who need it.

Allyship is an ongoing journey, not a destination. It requires continued learning, self-reflection, and a commitment to building a more loving world.
